  • Gronchi, I. and Entsminger, J. (2026). Towards mission theory: A systematic literature review of its critiques and avenues for progress (2010–2025). Research Policy, 55(10), 105634. doi
    Abstract

    During the last 15 years, researchers and practitioners alike showed a newfound interest in the potential of ‘mission-oriented’ innovation policy to tackle current societal challenges. In this period, the approach prompted multiple reflections and critiques from orthodox and heterodox scholars alike. However, a structured appraisal of this debate has been missing. Untangling its analytical complexity, this paper presents a systematic literature review of 224 articles and book chapters published between 2010 and 2025 to appraise the state-of-the-art debate within ‘mission theory’. Using the PRISMA Protocol and abductive thematic analysis, the paper identifies 18 themes mapped across two analytical dimensions: positioning (identifying either ‘external’ challenges or ‘internal’ gaps within mission theory); and layering (identifying challenges and gaps based on a ‘foundational’, ‘theoretical’, or ‘empirical’ ground). Building on the findings, the paper reflects on the sociological conditions preventing the progress of mission theory in contemporary research efforts – ultimately calling for greater epistemological reflexivity in the strategies adopted by researchers and practitioners to develop avenues for its further progress.

  • Gronchi, I. (2026). How to craft a mission? A Theory of Action for the strategic public management of mission-oriented innovation policy. Research Policy, 55(10), 105631. doi
    Abstract

    This paper explores the role of strategic public management within mission-oriented innovation policy. While recent scholarship has emphasised multiple aspects of missions, the distinct relevance of strategic public management in their implementation remains under-explored. To address this gap, the paper performs two complementary tasks. First, it uses a qualitative meta-synthesis of the relevant innovation literature to articulate a new Theory of Action for the analysis of missioncraft: namely, the institutional work public managers perform to create or maintain (disrupt) institutional logics supporting (hinder) their effective implementation. Second, it leverages a pre-study of the extant literature to illustrate the Theory of Action with three historical vignettes showing how the ToA can be applied at different scales and under different institutional logics (US' DARPA; China's NEV Plan programme; and EU's RIS3). Overall, the paper argues that the implementation of mission-oriented innovation policy requires public managers to adopt three critical strategic roles: i) enabling strategic learning across the public sector organisations leading the mission; ii) enabling dynamic accountability in the portfolio of projects supported by the mission; and iii) enabling collaborative innovation among the societal actors partaking the mission. By shifting the focus on the micro-foundations of mission implementation from structural underpinnings (i.e., legal, organisational, managerial) to agentic functions (i.e., strategic roles), the paper provides a new diagnostic lens for innovation policy research and practice – reflecting whether and how public managers can support mission-oriented innovation policy in light of the context-specific opportunities and constraints they face in their institutional work.

  • Gronchi, I. (2026). Crafting missions in practice: a formative assessment of public management in European net-zero policy. Policy Design and Practice, 1–22. doi
    Abstract

    Despite nearly a decade of engagement with mission-oriented policy, European governments struggle to translate its promises into real outcomes. This paper aims to address this implementation gap by proposing a formative assessment framework centered on missioncraft: the institutional work that public managers perform to support their implementation. Drawing on 54 interviews and official documentation covering 34 net-zero missions led by 12 European governments, the paper identifies three pathways through which public managers pursue mission progress: interventionist, facilitative, and hybrid. The analysis shows that mission-oriented policy implementation is best understood as an experimentalist process shaped by continuous administrative engagement with institutional barriers, rather than as the linear execution of a fixed political design. By linking formative assessment to the everyday practice of public management, the proposed framework provides a diagnostic tool to detect implementation gaps, support strategic learning, and inform adaptive managerial responses.

  • Cappellano, F., Gronchi, I., Ingstrup, M. B., Korsgaard, S., and Molica, F. (forthcoming). Understanding mission management: A multi-focal approach for mission-oriented innovation policy. Science and Public Policy. Accepted, in press.
    Abstract

    Mission-oriented innovation policy (MOIP) emerged as a key approach to addressing complex societal challenges. Despite widely adopted, MOIP remains difficult to implement effectively. This paper proposes a new agenda for mission management by integrating three complementary analytical lenses imported from strategic management: contingency theory, micro-foundational theory, and paradox theory. Rather than offering a singular framework, the paper demonstrates that each lens illuminates a key dimension of the challenges public managers face in designing and delivering MOIP – its external embeddedness, internal preconditions, and dynamic tensions – while compensating for each other's limitations. Bridging, the three lenses, the paper argues that mission management is best understood as a form of meta-governance: a reflexive, higher-order practice of calibrating the conditions under which diverse public, private, and societal actors pursue the transformative goals set within MOIP. By surfacing this insight, the paper thus illuminates a central normative tension in MOIP: exercising broad discretionary authority, mission managers can either entrench technocratic closure or deepen democratic ownership of the societal change sought by MOIP. Accordingly, the paper concludes by outlining a research agenda and practical implications for the democratic anchorage of mission management.
