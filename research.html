research

Since the 2015 Paris Agreement, Europe attempted to decarbonise its economy through ‘missions’: ambitious, goal-driven policies that promise to transform whole industrial sectors. However, many have fallen short of expectations. Research shows that macro-financial and meso-institutional factors played a huge role in hampering the European green transition. Yet, it remains unclear why European net-zero innovation policy has so often turned into mission-washing: bold political rhetoric without the administrative change needed to deliver it.

My doctoral thesis, ‘Why missions fail’, aims to close this gap by unearthing the role of public management in turning such bold political mandates into everyday administrative practice. First, it includes a Qualitative Comparative Analysis based on a survey of 34 missions led by 12 European governments. Second, it includes a comparative process tracing of four most different case studies: Danish agri-food, Dutch chemistry, British construction, French steel. As a result, it offers a formative assessment of European net-zero mission-oriented policy (2015-2025) based on 232 interviews, 152 documents and 12 focus groups. While current policy research explains mission-washing by inadequate bureaucratic structures or dynamic capabilities, the thesis finds an alternative explanation in the breakdown of political-administrative coordination.

Across Europe, political leaders have boxed in net-zero missions within the innovation policy toolkit. As subsidised green technologies faced barriers to diffusion, public managers sought to legitimise a more joined-up approach to public action by close engagement with an ever wider range of societal stakeholders and reaching far into regulation, infrastructure, and investment policy. However, in the absence of political leadership, the impact of their work remained largely peripheral. If this diagnosis is correct, the implication is that the long-term success of European net-zero missions will hinge not on better mission design or resource endowment, but rather on a new political settlement capable of expanding the mandate of such missions and the role of public managers in their implementation.

The monograph includes a diagnostic framework for the formative assessment of mission-oriented policy implementation; the first systematic comparative account of the European net-zero mission-oriented policy implementation; and three policy recommendations. Its content is currently being published across a range of peer-reviewed articles.
